\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Three Recurring Questions}
\label{sec:intro:eras}

Game theory first asks whether a solution exists. Von Neumann showed that every
finite two-person zero-sum game has a value together with a pair of optimal
mixed strategies~\cite{Neumann1928}, and von Neumann and Morgenstern placed that
result inside a general theory of economic behavior~\cite{vNM1944}. Nash
extended equilibrium existence to finite games with any finite number of players
and general payoff interactions~\cite{Nash1951}, well beyond strictly
competitive play.

Mechanism design makes the rules themselves the object of choice. In
the standard quasilinear setting, the Vickrey--Clarke--Groves family showed how
to make truthful reporting a dominant strategy by charging each participant for
the externality it imposes~\cite{Vickrey1961,Clarke1971,Groves1973}, and
Myerson characterized the
revenue-maximizing auction for the single-item Bayesian
setting~\cite{Myerson1981}. Impossibility results identify combinations of
domain restrictions and desiderata that cannot be satisfied
simultaneously~\cite{Arrow1951,Gibbard1973,Satterthwaite1975}.
Together, such results frame mechanism design as the selection of rules under
explicit incentive and feasibility constraints.

Existence and characterization results leave a computational question: how can
the solution be found? Algorithmic game theory studies this question for
different game representations and solution concepts
~\cite{NisanRoughgardenTardosVazirani2007}. Computing a Nash
equilibrium is PPAD-complete in general, and remains so with only two
players~\cite{DaskalakisGoldbergPapadimitriou2009,ChenDengTeng2009}. Even
classical constructive procedures can follow exponentially long
paths~\cite{LemkeHowson1964,SavaniVonStengel2006}. Established approaches weaken
the solution concept or exploit a restricted game's structure, often while
accepting approximation.

\subsection{How Learning Changes Computation}
\label{sec:intro:change}

Learning can redistribute computation across related instances. A system is
trained on many related cases and reuses the resulting conditional rule when a
new case arrives. This is the familiar structure of amortized optimization and
inference~\cite{Amos2022Amortized,KingmaWelling2014}, and in strategic settings
it has produced strong empirical systems. Neural counterfactual values and
continual re-solving produced expert-level play in heads-up no-limit
poker~\cite{Moravcik2017,BrownSandholm2018,BrownReBeL2020}. Self-play with
learned policy and value networks mastered board games from their rules, and
league training produced a StarCraft II agent at grandmaster
level~\cite{Silver2018,Vinyals2019}. Neural networks now also search spaces of
auction mechanisms that no analyst could
enumerate~\cite{Duetting2019,Duan2023,Duetting2024}.

In these examples, training is part of the solver: it shares work across related
instances and can reduce the cost of each later one. Learned systems also
participate in the interactions being studied. Pricing algorithms interact in
real markets~\cite{Calvano2020,Assad2024}. Language models negotiate and
coordinate~\cite{Bianchi2024NegotiationArena,Akata2025}, while alignment
procedures train one model against the responses of another
~\cite{Christiano2017,Ouyang2022}.
In each case the system's outputs can change what other participants do and
which situations the system later faces. Performance on a fixed set of cases
does not by itself establish performance after those responses. Game-theoretic
deviation tests provide one relevant standard: whether a participant can gain
by deviating within a specified class.

\subsection{Construction and Validity}
\label{sec:intro:cost}

Learned strategic methods are typically assessed through finite search or test
populations. RegretNet reports the largest gain found by gradient search over
sampled valuation profiles. Poker systems report performance against a test
population, while pricing audits estimate regret against alternatives
recoverable from deployment logs. Each result provides evidence for sampled cases and
accessible alternatives. Some target properties quantify over a larger
deviation class. For example, they require that no deviation in the specified
class be profitable or that truthful reporting yield at least as much utility as
every admissible misreport for every type. For such
deviation-defined properties, one profitable deviation refutes the claim.
Failure to find one supports the full claim only if the search covers the target
class or an additional argument controls what the search omitted. Figure~\ref{fig:candidate-counterexample} summarizes this asymmetry between
finite counterexample search and a claim over the full deviation class.

We distinguish two forms of validity.

\begin{description}
\item[Output validity.] Does the learned output have the claimed strategic
property? The answer depends on how the game and candidate can be queried or
checked.

\item[System validity.] Does a property established inside a designed game
support the objective that the game was designed to serve? For an alignment procedure, oversight protocol,
or market institution, this depends on how the designed interaction represents
both the participants and the objective.
\end{description}

The available access determines what further argument is possible. When each
alternative can be evaluated through an explicit model or simulator, the main
obstacle may be the size of the class. We call this a coverage limit. Broader
search or a bound derived from structure may address it.
With observational data, distinct strategic models can instead generate the
same realized behavior. This identification limit cannot be resolved by more
data from the same observational regime. It requires informative variation,
intervention, or maintained restrictions~\cite{HaileHortacsuKosenok2008}. Figure~\ref{fig:validity-gates} summarizes these two validity questions and
the implication that must hold in each case.

\subsection{Why the Distinction Matters}
\label{sec:intro:why}

The distinction becomes consequential when learned strategic systems leave a
fixed benchmark. Field evidence from retail gasoline markets links algorithmic
pricing to higher margins~\cite{Assad2024}. In oligopoly experiments,
language-model agents reach supra-competitive prices without
instruction~\cite{FishGonczarowskiShorrer2024}. Language models also propose
executable mechanisms and equilibrium derivations
~\cite{PrimeNash2025,LegoNE2025,LiuGuoConitzer2025}. These outputs can be
checked mechanically against a formal specification. Executability alone does
not supply that specification or establish the strategic claim.

The same evidentiary question appears in proposals to regulate algorithmic
collusion. These proposals ask whether a firm can produce evidence that its
pricing algorithm was not exploiting its rivals, with the burden of producing
that evidence assigned by
rule~\cite{HartlineLongZhang2024,HartlineWangZhang2025,Harrington2018}. When a
statistical property of a deployment log is offered as a legal defense, the
scope of the claim supported by that finite evidence becomes part of the
proposed regulatory design.

\subsection{Scope and How to Read This Survey}
\label{sec:intro:scope}

\paragraph{Scope.} We study systems that learn strategic rules from data or
interaction. A high-dimensional trained rule may require empirical queries
and statistical bounds, whereas executable code or membership in a structured
family may permit direct checking. The appropriate analysis therefore depends
on the representation and the property being claimed.

\paragraph{Related surveys and exclusions.} Stackelberg
and security games, mean-field games, multi-agent reinforcement learning beyond
its use as a response oracle, autobidding, and incentive design for federated
learning fall outside our selected domains. Wellman et al.\ and Curry et al.\ give
denser accounts of empirical game-theoretic analysis and automated mechanism
design, respectively~\cite{WellmanTuylsGreenwald2025,CurryAMDSurvey2025}. Sun et
al.\ survey the game theory--LLM intersection with the language model as the focal
technology~\cite{SunWuChengChu2025}. Podimata surveys incentive-aware machine
learning, where individuals adapt their inputs to a deployed decision
rule~\cite{Podimata2025}. Each of these surveys organizes the literature around
its object of study. We organize by the relation between a computation, the claim attached
to it, and the evidence available for that claim.

\paragraph{Organization.} Section~\ref{sec:recap} introduces the computational
background. Sections~\ref{sec:ai4gt} and~\ref{sec:gt4ai} organize six application
areas around the two validity questions. Section~\ref{sec:synthesis} states when
the two can be combined. Figure~\ref{fig:survey-route} maps the two main parts
of the survey to the six application areas.

\paragraph{Strategic response after deployment.} Both validity questions range
over a set of cases. After deployment, participants' responses can change the
inputs and counterparties represented in the observed data. Related
distributional feedback is formalized in performative
prediction~\cite{Perdomo2020Performative}. Generalization under strategic
response concerns whether the relevant property remains valid over cases
selected partly by those responses. A
guarantee on a fixed evaluation distribution need not extend to this deployment
distribution. The relevant response process and case domain must therefore be
part of the claim.
