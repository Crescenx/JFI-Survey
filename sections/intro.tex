\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A pricing algorithm can pass every audit on its own deployment log and still be
exploiting its rivals. A trained auction can post the highest estimated revenue
found anywhere in the mechanism class its search covered and still not be the
revenue-maximizing one. In each case a
learned system produces something that looks right on the evidence gathered so
far, yet the strategic property actually claimed for it --- competition,
optimality, truthfulness --- reaches beyond that evidence. This gap between what
finite evaluation shows and what a game-theoretic claim asserts is the subject of
this survey.

The two fields meeting at this intersection ask their
questions in different orders. Game theory begins by asking whether a solution exists.
\Citet{Neumann1928} showed that every finite two-person zero-sum game has a value
together with a pair of optimal mixed strategies, and \citet{vNM1944}
incorporated this result into a general theory of economic behavior.
\citet{Nash1951} extended equilibrium existence to finite games with any finite
number of players and general payoff interactions, well beyond strictly
competitive play. Mechanism design then asks which rules of interaction to
choose. In the standard quasilinear setting, the Vickrey--Clarke--Groves family
made truthful reporting a dominant strategy by charging each participant for the
externality it imposes~\citep{Vickrey1961,Clarke1971,Groves1973}, and
\citet{Myerson1981} characterized the revenue-maximizing auction for the
single-item Bayesian setting. Impossibility results mark the outer boundary,
identifying combinations of domain restrictions and desired properties that no rule can
satisfy at once~\citep{Arrow1951,Gibbard1973,Satterthwaite1975}.

Only after a solution concept exists does the computational question arise: how
does one find a solution? Algorithmic game theory studies exactly this, across
different game representations and solution
concepts~\mbox{\citep{NisanRoughgardenTardosVazirani2007}}. Computing a Nash equilibrium is PPAD-complete in general, and stays so with
only two players~\citep{DaskalakisGoldbergPapadimitriou2009,ChenDengTeng2009}.
PPAD is the class of total search problems whose solutions are guaranteed to
exist yet may be hard to find. Even classical constructive procedures can
follow exponentially long paths~\citep{LemkeHowson1964,SavaniVonStengel2006}. Established approaches recover
tractability by weakening the solution concept or exploiting a restricted game's
structure, usually while accepting some approximation. This is where learning, and more broadly artificial intelligence (AI), enters:
as a way to make that computation cheaper, and increasingly as a strategic
actor on its own.

\subsection{How Learning Changes Computation}
\label{sec:intro:change}

The first role is amortization: learning spreads the cost of computation across
many related problems. An amortized method trains a conditional rule on a family
of related cases and then reuses that rule on a new one, instead of solving each case from the beginning. This is the standard setup in amortized optimization and
inference~\citep{Amos2022Amortized,KingmaWelling2014}, and it also applies to
strategic systems. In heads-up no-limit poker, learned counterfactual values and continual
re-solving are among the ingredients of expert-level
play~\citep{Moravcik2017,BrownSandholm2018,BrownReBeL2020}. Self-play with
learned policy and value networks mastered board games from their rules, and
league training produced a StarCraft II agent at grandmaster
level~\citep{Silver2018,Vinyals2019}. Neural networks now even search spaces of
auction mechanisms too large for any analyst to
enumerate~\citep{Duetting2019,Duan2023,Duetting2024}.

In all of these the learned component is part of the solver: training shares
computation across instances and lowers the cost of solving later ones. But a
learned system can also become part of the interaction it was meant to analyze.
Pricing algorithms set prices in real
markets~\citep{Calvano2020,Assad2024}; large language models (LLMs) negotiate and
coordinate~\citep{Bianchi2024NegotiationArena,Akata2025}; preference-based
training turns human feedback on a model's own outputs into the signal that
shapes its policy, often through a learned reward
model~\citep{Christiano2017,Ouyang2022}. In this second role, the system's outputs change what
other participants do and which situations it will face next, so strong
performance on a fixed set of cases says little about performance once those
responses arrive. Game theory supplies the natural standard for that harder
question: can any participant gain by deviating within a specified class?

\subsection{Construction and Its Guarantee}
\label{sec:intro:cost}

That standard is exactly where finite evidence is not enough. Evidence for a
learning-based strategic method almost always comes from a finite search or a
test population. RegretNet reports the largest gain its gradient search found
over sampled valuation profiles~\citep{Duetting2019}; poker systems report play
against a test population~\citep{Moravcik2017,BrownSandholm2018,BrownReBeL2020};
pricing audits estimate regret against the alternatives recoverable from a
deployment log~\citep{HartlineLongZhang2024}. Each is genuine evidence for the cases sampled and the alternatives reached. The strategic property, though,
is typically stated over a wider class: that \emph{no} deviation in a specified
set is profitable, or that truthful reporting beats \emph{every} admissible
misreport of \emph{every} type. Against such a claim the logic is asymmetric. One
profitable deviation refutes it, but a search that finds none supports it only if the search covered the whole class, or if a separate argument bounds
the gain available outside what was searched.

Which argument is possible depends on how the object can be inspected. Two
questions follow, and they recur throughout the survey: the \emph{claim
question} (does the output hold the property?) and the \emph{goal question}
(does the property help the real goal?).

\begin{description}
\item[The claim question (does it hold?).] Does the learned output actually have the strategic property claimed for it? The answer depends on how the game and the candidate can be queried or checked.

\item[The goal question (does it help?).] Does a property established \emph{inside} a designed game deliver the objective that game was built to serve?
For an alignment procedure, an oversight protocol, or a market institution, this
depends on how faithfully the designed interaction represents both the participants
and the objective.
\end{description}

For the claim question, the kind of access decides the kind of obstacle. Sometimes every
alternative can be tried. A poker solver can evaluate any specified bluff, so the only
difficulty is that the bluffs are too many to enumerate: a \emph{coverage} limit, which broader
search or a structural bound can close. Other times the data are only
what happened to be observed. From a bidder's bids alone, two different valuations
can explain the same bidding equally well, and no amount of further data from the
same auction will distinguish them. This \emph{identification} limit is a different kind of problem: it can be resolved only by informative variation, intervention, or a maintained
assumption~\citep{HaileHortacsuKosenok2008}.

each with the implication it needs.

\subsection{Why the Distinction Matters}
\label{sec:intro:why}

The distinction becomes practical the moment a learned strategic system is judged
beyond a fixed benchmark. Field evidence from retail gasoline markets links
algorithmic pricing to higher margins~\citep{Assad2024}, and in oligopoly
experiments language-model agents reach supra-competitive prices with no
instruction to do so~\citep{FishGonczarowskiShorrer2024}. Language models also
propose executable mechanisms and equilibrium
derivations~\citep{PrimeNash2025,LegoNE2025,LiuGuoConitzer2025}; such outputs can
be checked mechanically against a formal specification, yet executability alone
neither supplies the specification nor establishes the strategic claim.

The same question about evidence appears in proposals to regulate algorithmic
collusion, which ask whether a firm can show that its pricing algorithm was not
exploiting its rivals, and assign the burden of that proof by
rule~\citep{HartlineLongZhang2024,HartlineWangZhang2025,Harrington2018}. Once a
statistical property of a deployment log is offered as a legal defense, the scope
of the claim that finite evidence can support becomes part of the regulatory
design itself.

\subsection{Scope and How to Read This Survey}
\label{sec:intro:scope}

\runin{Scope.} We study systems that learn strategic rules from data or
interaction. What counts as adequate evidence depends on the representation: a
high-dimensional trained rule may be reachable only through empirical queries and
statistical bounds, whereas executable code or membership in a structured family
may permit direct checking. The property being claimed and the way the rule can
be inspected together fix the appropriate analysis.

\runin{Related surveys and exclusions.} We leave aside Stackelberg and
security games, mean-field games, multi-agent reinforcement learning except where
it serves as a response oracle, autobidding, and incentive design for federated
learning. Complementary surveys cover neighboring ground with a different organizing
choice. \citet{WellmanTuylsGreenwald2025} and \citet{CurryAMDSurvey2025}
review empirical game-theoretic analysis and automated mechanism design, and
\citet{SunWuChengChu2025} survey the game-theory--LLM intersection with the
language model as the focal technology. \citet{Podimata2025} surveys
incentive-aware machine learning, where individuals adapt their inputs to a
deployed decision rule. Each organizes the literature around its object of study.
We instead organize around the relation between a computation, the claim
attached to it, and the evidence available for that claim.

\runin{Organization.} Section~\ref{sec:recap} introduces the computational
background. Section~\ref{sec:ai4gt} covers learning that \emph{computes} strategic solutions, and Section~\ref{sec:gt4ai} learning that \emph{acts} in the game; together they work through six application domains, laid out in Figure~\ref{fig:survey-route}. The two questions cut across both: the claim question runs through
Section~\ref{sec:ai4gt} and returns once the object acts, while the goal
question arises only after it does. Section~\ref{sec:synthesis} states when
they combine. One theme is common to all of them. After deployment, participants' responses can alter the very inputs and
counterparties a system meets; performative prediction is one formalization of
this feedback~\citep{Perdomo2020Performative}. A guarantee proved on a fixed
evaluation distribution therefore need not hold on the deployment distribution
those responses create. The relevant response process and case domain are therefore
part of the claim, not context around it.
