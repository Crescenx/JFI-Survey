\section{AI for Game Theory}
\label{sec:ai4gt}

This section examines the strategic properties attributed to learned outputs.
Equilibrium solvers often start from an explicit game or simulator, so individual
deviations can be evaluated even when the deviation class cannot be exhausted. Strategic
inference instead observes play generated by an unknown game and may leave
different latent explanations indistinguishable. A learned mechanism can be
queried on types and misreports before deployment, but its publication can also
change participation and the reports subsequently observed. The available
evidence therefore differs across the three settings.

\subsection{Constructive Equilibrium: Learning to Compute Strategic Solutions}
\label{sec:ai4gt:equilibrium}

An equilibrium condition quantifies over a deviation space. An explicit model or
simulator may evaluate any proposed deviation without making the entire class
searchable. Learned methods combine finite search with problem-specific analysis
to control what was omitted. Otherwise, the output is a candidate with a
residual measured over the evaluated deviations.

\subsubsection{Complexity and classical baseline}
The hardness results summarized in Section~\ref{sec:intro:eras} apply to general
finite games.
Two-player zero-sum games occupy a different computational regime because their
equilibrium conditions can be expressed as a primal--dual pair of linear programs. No-regret
dynamics provide another route: the time-averaged strategies of two players
with vanishing regret approach the minimax solution. This regret-to-equilibrium
link holds in two-player zero-sum games and does not extend unchanged to
general-sum play.

Classical numerical methods provide the reference case. A fixed algorithm is
already reusable across explicitly represented games, although its computation
is not adapted from a training distribution. In a finite bimatrix game, support
enumeration searches over possible supports and solves the associated
indifference and inequality conditions. The Lemke--Howson algorithm expresses
equilibrium as a linear complementarity problem and follows a
complementary-pivoting path to a fully labeled solution~\cite{LemkeHowson1964}.
Homotopy methods embed the game in a parameterized family and follow an
equilibrium path from an easier auxiliary problem. Gambit implements several of
these representations and algorithms~\cite{gambit}. These algorithms require
finite, fully represented games, and their worst-case cost remains substantial:
Lemke--Howson can follow an exponentially long path~\cite{SavaniVonStengel2006}.

\subsubsection{Learned quantities within equilibrium procedures}
Differentiable equilibrium methods construct a regularized response map and
minimize the residual between the current strategy and its regularized best
response. Smoothing permits policy-gradient updates and neural function
approximation, but it changes the target and introduces regularization bias.
NashPG fixes the regularization strength within each round and updates the
reference policy between rounds~\cite{NashPG2025}. The moving reference reduces
the bias accumulated across rounds.
The analysis gives a strictly monotone reduction in Bregman divergence to the
Nash-equilibrium set and convergence in two-player zero-sum extensive-form
games. NashPG uses sampled policy-gradient updates in large
imperfect-information domains, including Battleship and No-Limit Texas Hold'em.
The neural policy reuses a learned response rule across information states.
Liu, Farina, and Ozdaglar take a related policy-gradient route for two-player zero-sum
imperfect-information extensive-form games~\cite{LiuFarinaOzdaglar2025}.
Their method optimizes a regularized objective and establishes best-iterate
convergence to a regularized Nash equilibrium while avoiding the
importance-sampling step used by some policy-gradient estimators. The paper
proves a best-iterate result, which does not describe every later policy. The
convergence target is a regularized equilibrium and therefore retains the
approximation introduced by the regularizer.

Regret minimization compares
the learner's accumulated payoff with the payoff of an alternative strategy. If
average regret vanishes, no fixed deviation provides a persistent advantage in
hindsight. In a two-player zero-sum game, duality bounds the exploitability of
the average strategies by the players' average regrets. Averaging the learning
trajectory produces the equilibrium approximation in this setting.
Counterfactual regret minimization (CFR) applies the same idea to extensive-form games by
decomposing overall regret across information sets~\cite{Zinkevich2007}. At an
information set, counterfactual regret compares the selected action with its
alternatives, with the comparison weighted by the reach contribution of the
other players and chance. This assigns an update signal even when the player's current strategy
does not reach that information set. Regret matching converts positive
cumulative counterfactual regrets into the next strategy. In two-player
zero-sum games, the regret decomposition bounds the exploitability of the
averaged strategy.

Deep CFR replaces the tabular regrets with neural approximations in domains
where explicit storage is impractical~\cite{BrownDeepCFR2019}. It samples game
trees through external-sampling traversals, stores regret and strategy
observations in replay memories, and trains neural networks to approximate the
corresponding functions. Neural approximation shares information across related
states, with sampling and function-approximation error added to the original
CFR analysis. These errors enter the exploitability bound through the regret
decomposition.

Return-maximizing self-play can produce strong play, as in AlphaZero, without
directly minimizing an equilibrium residual. Its empirical guarantee concerns
the training population, which may omit a profitable counterstrategy or permit
cycling among mutually exploitable policies. Equilibrium-oriented methods tie
the objective or stopping condition to an equilibrium residual or a response
oracle.

In general-sum games, independent external-regret minimization does not generally converge to Nash equilibrium. It ensures that the empirical joint-action distribution approaches the set of coarse correlated equilibria. Stronger internal or swap-regret guarantees lead to correlated equilibrium. These concepts use different deviation classes. A coarse correlated equilibrium rules out choosing a fixed alternative action before observing a recommendation, whereas a correlated equilibrium rules out deviations that condition on the recommended action. Neither condition requires the joint distribution to factor into independent player strategies, so both are weaker than Nash equilibrium.

This substitution is computationally useful because, in explicitly represented
finite games, correlated and coarse correlated equilibria admit linear
descriptions and can be verified in polynomial time. It also changes the target:
convergence to a coarse correlated
equilibrium does not solve the original Nash problem. The no-regret theorem and
the selected deviation class determine the result. Meta-learned CFR trains a
parameterized regret minimizer with a strategy-correlation meta-objective that
moves coarse-correlated-equilibrium learning toward lower Nash
exploitability~\cite{Sychrovsky2025}. Its meta-loss measures the remaining
distance to the stronger Nash condition.

\subsubsection{Learned responses for empirical games}
Empirical game-theoretic analysis approximates the strategic problem through a
finite policy population. When the underlying game is defined by a simulator,
its complete normal form may be too large to enumerate. The analyst selects a
strategy set, estimates its payoff profiles, and solves the resulting empirical
game~\cite{WellmanTuylsGreenwald2025}.
PSRO makes the population adaptive. It solves the current empirical game,
trains an approximate response to its meta-strategy, adds that policy to the
population, and estimates the new payoff entries~\cite{Lanctot2017}. Pipeline
PSRO runs several response processes and empirical-game updates concurrently
to improve scalability~\cite{McAleerPipelinePSRO2020}.
Multi-agent reinforcement learning supplies the response oracle, while the
empirical-game solver coordinates policies already found.

JPSRO retains this population structure and uses correlated-equilibrium
meta-solvers~\cite{Marris2021}. At each iteration, a deep reinforcement-learning
oracle searches for responses to a distribution derived from the empirical
game. Max-Gini selection chooses a high-entropy correlated or coarse correlated
equilibrium among the available solutions. The output is a CE or CCE of the
empirical game. Across these methods, extending the empirical solution to the
underlying game requires accurate payoff estimates and coverage of relevant
deviations outside the current population. For PSRO variants, that coverage
depends on the response oracle.

\subsubsection{Learned continuation values for online re-solving}
Large imperfect-information games can defer some computation until play reaches
a subgame. A solver constructs a blueprint and refines it online, with value
bounds or resolving constraints controlling the additional exploitability at
the subgame boundary. DeepStack demonstrated this architecture in heads-up no-limit poker~\cite{Moravcik2017}. It continually re-solves the public portion of the
game and uses a neural counterfactual-value function beyond a limited lookahead.
The network compresses continuation computation across public states;
continual re-solving and counterfactual-value bounds control additional
exploitability. Libratus combines a CFR-based blueprint with nested safe subgame
solving and endgame refinement, without neural function approximation in its
safe-resolving component~\cite{BrownSandholm2018}. Both systems explicitly
control the exploitability introduced by local re-solving.

ReBeL represents an imperfect-information game through public belief states
~\cite{BrownReBeL2020}. Each state combines public information with a distribution
over the players' private states, producing a continuous-state
perfect-information formulation on which reinforcement learning and search can
operate. ReBeL trains policy and value networks through self-play and uses them
within nested subgame solving. For two-player zero-sum games, the analysis proves
convergence to Nash equilibrium under the prescribed representation and solving
assumptions. Private information remains hidden. The public belief state is
sufficient for the recursive calculation.

\subsubsection{Symbolic proposals under executable checks}
Language models can propose symbolic objects such as equations or code for an
individual game. A formal or computational component must then check feasibility and the
relevant deviation conditions. Subject to the soundness of its encoding and
checker, this mechanical test can establish the property independently of how
the proposal was generated. PrimeNash follows this division of labor
for analytical equilibrium characterization~\cite{PrimeNash2025}. Its agents generate candidate strategies
and symbolic derivations, then use executable tools to manipulate and test the
expressions. The target is an instance-specific symbolic characterization,
including parameter regions and boundary cases. Explicit best-response
conditions and machine-executable checks support the claimed equilibrium.

The same division applies one level up, where the object generated is an
algorithm rather than a solution. LegoNE applies language-model generation to
the synthesis of equilibrium algorithms~\cite{LegoNE2025}. Expert proof strategies are represented in a
domain-specific language, and each generated algorithm is compiled into a
finite constrained optimization problem. Solving that problem determines a
worst-case approximation guarantee for the encoded game class and objective.

\medskip
\noindent
Across these methods, learned parameters amortize computation across states or
games. Their equilibrium guarantees come from the surrounding analysis:
duality, regret decomposition, safe resolving, or verified constrained
optimization. Total cost includes data generation and training, and remaining
approximation should be reported as exploitability or regret.

\subsection{Strategic Inference: What Observed Play Can Identify}
\label{sec:ai4gt:inference}

Strategic inference starts with realized histories and actions rather than a
known game. Counterfactual behavior may be unidentified: two latent games can
induce the same distribution along the realized paths. Additional data from that
regime cannot separate them. Haile, Hortaçsu, and Kosenok show a
more basic falsifiability problem. Even strong prior restrictions on unobserved
payoff disturbances leave a general quantal-response specification able to
rationalize any distribution of behavior in a single normal-form
game~\cite{HaileHortacsuKosenok2008}. QRE acquires empirical content only through
further maintained assumptions, such as restrictions stable across games or a
more specific response function.

Even a falsifiable model may be only partially recoverable from the data. In
multi-agent inverse reinforcement learning, a single observed equilibrium is
typically consistent with many reward functions. Within the entropy-regularized
class they study, Freihaut and Ramponi show that a
given reward induces a unique quantal response equilibrium, which removes the
equilibrium-selection ambiguity without making the feasible reward set a
singleton~\cite{FreihautRamponi2025}. The resulting object is an identified set
whose width records the remaining ambiguity. Informative variation can shrink
this set. Cross-game or within-participant variation may separate explanations
that coincide along one realized path. The model restrictions determine which
behavioral differences would be informative, and the data must contain those
differences.

Learned methods place the assumptions needed for identification at different
stages of the analysis. Some supply the response model itself. GameNet learns a map from a two-player
normal-form payoff matrix to a distribution over actions, with an architecture
that is equivariant to action relabeling and contains layers intended to
represent level-$0$ behavior and finitely many levels of iterated
response~\cite{Hartford2016}. ElementaryNet exposes an ambiguity in that
interpretation. GameNet's nominal level-$0$ component can itself express
strategic responses. ElementaryNet replaces it with one formally unable to do
so and, after adding explicit higher-level modules, recovers statistically
indistinguishable predictive performance~\cite{DEon2026ElementaryNet}. The
comparable predictive performance does
not validate the behavioral interpretation originally assigned to GameNet's
internal modules. Related work supplies theory through the training data by
pretraining on synthetic choices from cognitive models before fine-tuning on
human observations~\cite{Bourgin2019}.

Others fix a response model and estimate the latent quantities inside it. Let
$D$ denote the observed games, histories, and actions, distributed as
$P_{\phi,\theta}$, where $\theta$ collects payoffs, private types, chance
parameters, or relations among agents, and $\phi$ the remaining parameters of
the response model. Inference may return a
point estimate, feasible set, or posterior. A differentiable
regularized-equilibrium layer can pass prediction error back to game
parameters~\cite{Ling2018}. In auction data, dominance restrictions turn
observed offers into feasible valuation intervals that enter the loss of a
learned history-dependent response model~\cite{CuiYu2023}. A generative theory of
social relations instead permits Bayesian inverse planning over latent
relationships~\cite{Shum2019}.

A third group searches over symbolic response models. AutoBM proposes them in
natural language and code, compiles and fits them, and uses held-out likelihood
for selection~\cite{AutoBM2026}. Because selection still relies on observed fit,
models that predict the same observations remain indistinguishable. Downstream
decisions should then account for the compatible set rather than treat the
selected model as identified.

\subsection{Mechanism Design: Learning Rules under Incentive Constraints}
\label{sec:ai4gt:mechanism}

A learned mechanism is evaluated both for its pre-deployment incentive properties
and for its post-deployment performance. Incentive compatibility covers all
relevant types and admissible misreports, including reports absent from the
sample. Finite training and evaluation therefore establish this property only
when a valid theorem or bound controls the omitted cases. The
phrase ``outside the training distribution'' can refer to two distinct
extrapolations:

\begin{itemize}
\item From \emph{sampled truthful type profiles} to the deployment type
distribution. This affects objectives computed in expectation --- revenue,
welfare --- and is the ordinary generalization problem.
\item From \emph{searched misreports} to all profitable deviations. This affects
the incentive claim itself, and it is not an averaging problem: a single
unexamined misreport that pays is a counterexample, not a tail event.
\end{itemize}

Write \(u_i(v_i, b_i, b_{-i})\) for the utility of agent \(i\) whose true type is
\(v_i\), who reports \(b_i\), and whose opponents report \(b_{-i}\); the first
argument is the true type, and the remaining arguments are the submitted reports.
For a direct mechanism, dominant-strategy incentive compatibility then requires
truthful reporting to dominate every misreport, for every type of every agent and
every profile of opponent reports:
\[
u_i(v_i,v_i,b_{-i})
\;\geq\;
u_i(v_i,b_i',b_{-i}),
\]
for all agents \(i\), all types \(v_i\), all alternative reports \(b_i'\), and all
opponent profiles \(b_{-i}\). The condition is counterfactual. It compares
utility under truthful reporting with utility under alternative reports,
including reports that may never appear in the training data. A single
profitable misreport rejects incentive compatibility, whereas acceptance
requires the inequality to hold over the full type and misreport spaces.

During training, an optimizer or misreporter network can submit alternative
reports to the candidate mechanism and evaluate their utility on sampled types.
Some methods optimize the resulting empirical regret in a flexible mechanism
class. Others restrict the representation or add post-training analysis to cover
the full type and report spaces. Shared parameters reuse allocation and payment
computations across type profiles. The remaining costs arise from the
representation and validation beyond the training sample.

\subsubsection{Guarantees from explicit constraints: the classical baseline}

Automated mechanism design provides the computational baseline. In a finite
type space, a designer can introduce allocation and payment variables for each
reported type profile and optimize expected revenue or social welfare.
Feasibility, individual rationality, and incentive compatibility appear as
explicit linear or mixed-integer constraints~\cite{ConitzerSandholm2002,CurryAMDSurvey2025}.

The direct formulation also exposes its scaling limit. The number of allocation
and payment variables grows with the cardinality of the agents' joint type space, while
incentive compatibility adds comparisons between truthful and alternative
reports. Continuous types usually require discretization. The resulting program
enforces the constraints at the selected grid points unless an additional
argument controls behavior between them; finer grids reduce this error while
increasing the program size.

\subsubsection{Guarantees from searched violations: incentive compatibility as a loss}

Neural mechanism design replaces the table of type-contingent outcomes with parameterized allocation and payment functions. RegretNet is the central instance of this move~\cite{Duetting2019}. Given a reported valuation profile, one network outputs an allocation and another determines payments. The parameters are trained to maximize expected revenue while controlling violations of incentive compatibility and individual rationality. For bidder \(i\), ex-post regret at a valuation profile can be written as
\[
r_i(v)
=
\max_{b_i'}
\left[
u_i(v_i,b_i',v_{-i})
-
u_i(v_i,v_i,v_{-i})
\right].
\]
The mechanism is incentive compatible when this quantity is zero for every bidder and every valuation profile. RegretNet estimates the maximization through gradient-based searches over misreports and incorporates the resulting regret into an augmented-Lagrangian training objective.

The mechanism network proposes allocations and payments, and the inner search
returns profitable misreports as both violations and training signals. ALGNet
replaces this inner optimization with a learned misreporter and evaluates regret
over deviations that the misreporter can represent and find~\cite{Rahme2021Auction}.
In the resulting two-player game, the auctioneer changes the mechanism to
increase revenue while the misreporter searches for reports that increase an
agent's utility.

Other methods retain empirical regret while changing the architecture or design
objective. RegretFormer adds attention and an explicit regret budget
~\cite{Ivanov2022}, giving the admissible empirical regret a direct role in the
objective.
EquivariantNet uses bidder and item permutation symmetries to share computation
across relabeled auction instances~\cite{Rahme2021Equivariant}.
CITransNet extends the transformer formulation to contextual and variable-scale
auction environments~\cite{Duan2022}. Its permutation-equivariant architecture
can accommodate changes in the numbers and attributes of market participants.
PreferenceNet changes the design objective by learning constraints associated
with fairness and allocation diversity from representative
examples~\cite{Peri2021}. None of these architectural changes establishes
incentive compatibility for unseen reports. The supporting evidence remains
empirical: regret is evaluated on sampled types and searched misreports.

\subsubsection{Guarantees by construction: menus and affine maximizers}

Some methods restrict the learned mechanism to a family whose economic structure
already supports truthful behavior. Menu-based mechanisms illustrate the
approach. A menu specifies a collection of allocation--payment pairs, and a
bidder selects the option that maximizes its utility under its true valuation.
The designer therefore need not learn a separate outcome for every possible
report. MenuNet searches over such menus for single-bidder auction
environments~\cite{Shen2019}. RochetNet similarly parameterizes a utility or menu
representation designed around the characterization of truthful single-bidder
mechanisms~\cite{Duetting2019}. In these systems, the network optimizes the
quality of the menu, while bidder-optimal selection provides the economic basis
for truthfulness. Approximation may affect how close the learned mechanism's
revenue is to the optimum, but the source of the incentive guarantee differs from an
empirical regret penalty.

Affine-maximizer auctions provide a related structural family for multiple bidders. An affine maximizer selects an allocation by maximizing a weighted sum of reported valuations together with allocation-specific offsets. With the corresponding payment rule, the mechanism inherits dominant-strategy incentive compatibility and individual rationality from the affine-maximizer construction. AMenuNet learns the weights, offsets, and a scalable set of candidate allocations instead of searching unrestricted allocation and payment functions~\cite{Duan2023}. The neural model enlarges the practical search over affine-maximizer mechanisms, while the classical mechanism class determines the strategic property. Restricting the representation may exclude the unconstrained revenue-optimal mechanism, but a candidate inside the class does not need empirical regret minimization to obtain its basic truthfulness result.

Moving from single-bidder auctions or restricted affine-maximizer families to general multi-bidder auctions introduces a compatibility problem. Each bidder can be viewed as facing a menu conditional on the other bidders' reports, but these menus must jointly correspond to a feasible allocation and payment rule. GemNet formalizes this menu-compatibility requirement for multi-bidder auctions~\cite{Duetting2024}. Its training procedure penalizes violations of compatibility, and its post-training construction modifies prices so that the learned menus define a strategy-proof mechanism. Lipschitz regularity then bounds the error introduced by checking only a finite valuation grid.

BundleFlow extends menu learning to combinatorial auctions with substantially
larger item spaces~\cite{WangBundleFlow2025}. An unrestricted menu is
exponentially large because every subset of items can define a distinct option.
BundleFlow uses a learned flow or diffusion-based procedure to generate relevant
bundles and their menu entries without enumerating the complete bundle space.
Bidder-optimal selection keeps the generated menu truthful, while omitted
bundles and learned prices determine its approximation error.

A different frontier removes discretization from the outcome space. Ma et al.\ parameterize
pricing rules with Partial GroupMax Networks~\cite{TEDI2025}. The architecture admits only rules
that satisfy the stated truthfulness conditions over continuous outcomes;
training selects among them.

\subsubsection{Guarantees that can be re-checked: executable mechanisms}

A deployable mechanism must specify a rule whose feasibility and incentive
properties can be analyzed. LLM-AMD formulates automated mechanism design as the
generation of mechanism code~\cite{LiuGuoConitzer2025}. A language model
proposes an executable rule from a natural-language description of the
environment and design objective. A problem-specific fixing process then
revises the candidate to address feasibility and strategy-proofness violations.
The framework can recover canonical auction structures and produce
redistribution rules in the settings considered. Through
programming-by-example, it can also express a previously learned neural
mechanism as a more interpretable program. In either task, executable code makes
failures easier to inspect and search for, but the rule can still violate a
constraint on an untested input.

A published mechanism also changes the environment to which it is applied.
Participant responses can alter entry and the reports observed after
deployment, so the deployment distribution of reports may differ from the
sampled training distribution. The mechanism does not thereby determine the
underlying type distribution. Endogenous participation or type formation requires
an explicit model.
