\section{Synthesis and Outlook}
\label{sec:synthesis}

A pricing algorithm that passes every audit its own deployment log can support
has done everything asked of it. Nothing in that record is false, and the claim
is still not safe, because the property being asserted reached past the evidence
gathered for it.

The six domains surveyed here are an account of that reach: where it is short enough
to close by searching harder, where no amount of searching will close it, and
what has to be supplied instead. Learning has
changed which strategic computations are cheap. It has not changed what makes a
strategic claim true. Something outside the search has to carry the conclusion past
the cases actually tried: a duality argument, a regret decomposition,
membership in a truthful family, a protocol theorem, a maintained restriction, a
logged randomization. Where no such argument is available, the supportable claim is
confined to the domain the evidence covers.

\subsection{Combining the Claim and Goal Questions}
\label{sec:synthesis:compose}

To say that a learned system serves a real-world goal is to assert two implications:
its output has the claimed property, and that property, established inside the
designed game, delivers the external objective. The claim question tests the first;
the goal question tests the second.

Write $\widehat{x}$ for the learned output, $P_\Gamma$ for a property defined
inside a game $\Gamma$, and $Q$ for the external objective that the game
$\Gamma_Q$ was built to serve:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{claim question:}\qquad
&\widehat{x} \quad\Longrightarrow?\quad P_\Gamma(\widehat{x}),\\
\text{goal question:}\qquad
&P_{\Gamma_Q}(x) \quad\Longrightarrow?\quad Q(x),
\end{aligned}
\]
where $\Longrightarrow?$ marks an implication whose validity is itself the object
of study. The chain closes only when both links speak about the same object, in the same game, over the same domain, that is, when $x=\widehat{x}$ and
$\Gamma=\Gamma_Q$:
\[
\widehat{x} \;\Longrightarrow\; P_{\Gamma_Q}(\widehat{x}) \;\Longrightarrow\;
Q(\widehat{x}).
\]

That is a stricter requirement than it looks. It is where the argument usually
breaks, and the break goes unnoticed because each link can be perfectly sound
on its own.
Algorithmic pricing is the standard case. An algorithm can be shown, rigorously,
to maximize its own firm's profit: a theorem about the single-firm game. A
regulator's objective is defined in a different game: the joint market outcome, including
the consumers, who are affected parties and not participants
in the firm's optimization. Both proofs can be correct and the welfare claim
still fail to follow, because the property was never established in the game
where welfare is defined.

The two links can meet different limits, and the difference decides the remedy.
When a finite search covers only part of the relevant deviation class, it creates a
coverage limit. The best deviation found is a lower bound on the best that exists;
claiming that nothing better is available requires exhaustive coverage or a bound on
the unsearched class. An identification limit arises when distinct latent models are
observationally equivalent under the available regime and maintained assumptions.
More data from that same regime does not distinguish them.
Figure~\ref{fig:claim-obstacles} sets these two recurring limits side by side. Coverage and
identification constrain different forms of evidence, so the
appropriate remedy depends on how the candidate and game can be queried, which
variation is available, and which structural assumptions can be defended.

\subsection{What Each Domain Contributes}
\label{sec:synthesis:map}

The survey's organizing map assigns a primary limit to each of the six domains.
Those assignments identify the constraints foregrounded by the results reviewed here; they do not
exhaust every possible scientific failure in those domains. What changes across the
map is which limit is foregrounded and which further argument the target claim needs.

Within this map, equilibrium computation, mechanism design, and strategic evaluation
foreground coverage because every alternative can in principle be asked about and
there are too many to exhaust. These cases call for broader search, response
oracles, membership in a structured family, a bound that carries a finite grid
to a continuous domain. Strategic inference foregrounds identification. The bids are
what they are, several valuations explain them equally well, and only new
variation, an intervention, or a maintained restriction narrows the field.

The map assigns designed-game scope to games for training and oversight. The
central question is whether the designed game is the right one, and no evidence
gathered only inside it can settle that external fit. In the deployment-governance results surveyed here, coverage and
identification both arise. Addressing them takes exploration and logging to
expose counterfactuals, pessimistic completion for those that stay hidden, and
a welfare or legal benchmark supplied from outside the theorem altogether.

\subsection{Open Questions}
\label{sec:synthesis:open}

Four questions remain open across the domains surveyed here.

\runin{The unstated class.} Nearly every empirical number in this survey --- an
exploitability estimate, a win rate, a regret figure --- is defined against a
class of alternatives, and means little without it. That class is often left
implicit, and cost is reported the same way, with training and evaluation
separated from per-instance inference or omitted. The remedy at the level of a
single paper is easy to state: name the alternative class, say how it relates to
the target claim, and report the full cost. The open problem is that we have no
shared vocabulary for how far a reported number reaches, so results are not
comparable across papers even when they carry the same name.

\runin{What survives underdetermination.} When several latent explanations fit
the observed play equally well, the evidence identifies a set of compatible
explanations. The unresolved task is to determine which conclusions hold throughout
that set. Strategic inference has tools for this but uses them rarely; pessimistic
completion in deployment auditing is one worked example, applied to a missing
transcript rather than a missing preference.

\runin{Where the result actually lives.} Results about training and oversight
protocols hold under modeled bounds on what each participant can compute, see,
and verify. These bounds determine what the designed game establishes and should
appear in the main claim. The unresolved modeling question is which bounds describe
a human judge interacting with systems whose capabilities are themselves moving.

\runin{The domain chosen by the response.} A guarantee proved on a fixed
evaluation distribution need not survive deployment, because participants'
responses select the cases the system will actually meet. Evaluation domains that
account for this require a model, or an audit, of that selection. Additional test
cases from the fixed pre-release distribution do not identify the cases induced by
post-release strategic responses.

Whether an in-game strategic property supports deployment depends on the evidence
connecting it to the institution's objective. Proposals to place on a firm the
burden of showing that its pricing algorithm was not exploiting its rivals make the
choice of evidence standard explicit. That standard determines which strategic
claims can support deployment and who bears the remaining uncertainty when coverage
or identification is incomplete.
