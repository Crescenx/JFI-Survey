\section{Background}
\label{sec:recap}

Equilibrium analysis asks whether any player in a game could have done better by
doing something else. An equilibrium is the case where the answer is no: no player gains by changing
only its own action. Incentive compatibility puts the same question to truthful
reporting: no participant does better by misreporting its type than by reporting
it truthfully. That ``something else'' is a \emph{deviation}, and the set of deviations one insists on checking against (every alternative
action, or only a restricted family) is the \emph{deviation class}. It is the object this survey is built around, and it returns in every section that follows.

Two facts about it set the background. Checking is not free: establishing that
\emph{no} profitable deviation exists, over a large or implicitly described
class, can require solving a computationally hard optimization or search problem. And learning does not by itself answer it: training a rule across many related
games can produce a strong candidate cheaply, but a candidate is not a
guarantee. A learned output still needs a check or bound tied to the specific
property claimed for it.

\subsection{Equilibrium Conditions and Computational Demands}
\label{subsec:recap-gt}

For both equilibrium and incentive compatibility, the deviation class
determines what must be checked. This subsection states the two
deviation conditions in turn, separates constructing a candidate from verifying
it, and closes with the standard restrictions and relaxations that recover
tractability.

\subsubsection{Equilibrium and its deviation class}

The simplest case is also the oldest. The minimax theorem establishes that every
finite two-player zero-sum game has a value and a pair of optimal mixed
strategies~\citep{Neumann1928,vNM1944}. Rock-paper-scissors is the textbook example:
throwing each option a third of the time is unbeatable in expectation, and the value
is a tie. In such games a player's equilibrium strategy is also its worst-case-optimal
strategy, so equilibrium and robustness coincide, a coincidence special to strictly
competitive play that does not extend to general games.

A Nash equilibrium is a profile in which no player gains by deviating
alone~\citep{Nash1951}. To make ``gains'' precise, measure each player's best
unilateral improvement: for a profile $s$ and a class $\mathcal{D}_i$ of
deviations available to player $i$, the aggregate gap is
\[
  \mathrm{Gap}_{\mathcal{D}}(s)
  \;=\;
  \sum_i \Bigl[\, \sup_{s_i' \in \mathcal{D}_i} u_i(s_i', s_{-i}) - u_i(s) \,\Bigr].
\]
Take each $\mathcal{D}_i$ to include the player's current strategy. When
$\mathcal{D}_i$ is the whole strategy set, this sum is commonly called NashConv,
and closely related normalizations are called exploitability; it is zero exactly
at a Nash equilibrium. Shrink $\mathcal{D}_i$ to a restricted class and the gap reports only on deviations in that class: the same formula, a narrower
claim.

Two warnings come with this condition. The inequalities can be checked one player
at a time, holding the opponents fixed, but every player's inequality must hold at
the same joint profile. And the condition is static: it says nothing about
whether an adaptive process ever reaches such a profile, which is a separate
question of convergence.

Richer game forms enlarge the set of alternatives being compared, but never
change what the comparison is about. Extensive-form games represent the order of
play and each player's information; \citet{Kuhn1953} showed that under perfect
recall, mixed and behavioral strategies induce the same outcome distributions.
\citet{Selten1975} added refinements at contingencies the equilibrium path never
reaches, and \citet{Harsanyi1968} handled private information by making a player's
payoff-relevant characteristics a \emph{type}, turning incomplete information into
a Bayesian game. In each, the refinement changes the counterfactual alternatives,
while comparison against those alternatives remains the content of the concept. A
correlated equilibrium broadens the setting differently: a mediator draws a joint
action profile and privately recommends one action to each player, and no player
gains by deviating as a function of the recommendation it
receives~\citep{Aumann1974}. Quantal response equilibrium (QRE) relaxes the comparison
itself, replacing exact best response with a smooth rule under which better
actions are merely more likely, so mistakes persist~\citep{McKelveyPalfrey1995}.

\subsubsection{Design and deviation-based incentive constraints}

If equilibrium takes the game as given, mechanism design reverses the
question: it chooses the rules so that participants' incentives produce a desired
outcome. Impossibility results bound what any choice of rules can
achieve~\citep{Arrow1951,Gibbard1973,Satterthwaite1975}. Within those bounds, the
revelation principle lets the designer work with direct mechanisms, in which
agents report types and truthful reporting is an equilibrium.

Incentive compatibility is, once again, a statement about deviations: for every
relevant type, truthful reporting must do at least as well as \emph{every}
admissible alternative report. The deviation class is now the misreport space,
its scope set by the equilibrium concept and the mechanism's domain. Two classical results are central to the design side. The Vickrey--Clarke--Groves family
achieves efficient allocation in dominant
strategies~\citep{Vickrey1961,Clarke1971,Groves1973}, and \citet{Myerson1981}
characterized the revenue-optimal single-item Bayesian auction.

\subsubsection{Construction and checking}

Both conditions (equilibrium and incentive compatibility) are ultimately
verified by ruling out profitable deviations, and verifying is not the same
problem as constructing. That gap is where the difficulty lies. The PPAD-completeness results noted at
the start of the introduction concern total search problems: a solution is
guaranteed to exist, yet finding one may be
intractable~\citep{Papadimitriou1994}. Checking has an asymmetry that comes from the
deviation class. Given an explicit finite game and a rational profile, evaluating
one specified deviation is direct, and a single profitable deviation refutes the
equilibrium claim immediately. Establishing that \emph{no} profitable deviation
exists, over a large or implicitly represented class, is the harder direction, and
can require an optimization or a structural result. Mechanism design faces the
same difficulty, because optimizing over contingent rules is
itself a hard search problem~\citep{ConitzerSandholm2002}.

\subsubsection{Standard routes to tractability}

When the exact problem is too hard, two strategies recover tractability: weaken the
solution concept, or restrict the game class, usually while accepting a bounded residual gain. Correlated and coarse correlated equilibria illustrate the first
strategy. In explicitly represented finite games they admit linear descriptions and are
computable in polynomial time. They also arise from decentralized learning:
when every player in a repeated game drives external regret to zero, the
empirical distribution of play approaches the coarse correlated set, and
stronger regret notions reach the correlated
set~\citep{Hannan1957,HartMasColell2000,BlumMansour2007}. The deviation class is explicit in this result. The equilibrium notion one
obtains depends on which deviations the regret criterion compares
against~\citep{GordonGreenwaldMarks2008}. Each relaxation therefore changes
the claim for which a deductive guarantee is available. Learning composes with any of these
strategies, by fitting a conditional rule across related games and reusing it on later
instances.

\subsection{What Training Does}
\label{subsec:recap-dl}

The previous subsection fixed what must be checked and how costly checking can
be; this one turns to what training contributes. Learning helps because of one basic trade-off: it moves work off the per-instance solver and onto a
training phase whose cost is paid once and shared. This subsection first makes that trade-off precise, then names what training does \emph{not} provide: the guarantees that still
have to be established once training is done.

\subsubsection{Amortization: sharing computation across instances}

A per-instance solver is invoked afresh on each game. An amortized system instead fits a conditional rule across a family of related
cases and later evaluates that rule on a new case. Its total computation
includes both the training and the later evaluations, so its potential
advantage comes entirely from sharing the trained rule across cases. This is the structure of amortized optimization and
inference~\citep{Amos2022Amortized,KingmaWelling2014}, with different
architectures encoding different forms of sharing across states or instances.

Reinforcement learning adds the dependence that matters for strategic settings:
an action changes the data the learner will next
observe~\citep{SuttonBarto2018,Williams1992,SuttonPolicyGradient2000,Mnih2015}.
When the rules are known, self-play can create fresh training games as the
policy changes, the route used to train the policy and value networks in
board-game systems~\citep{Silver2018}. When behavior is observed but the objective
is hidden, inverse reinforcement learning estimates which objectives are
consistent with that behavior~\citep{NgRussell2000,HoErmon2016}. Preference-based
fine-tuning turns comparisons between outputs into the training signal, making the
learning target itself something another party
supplies~\citep{Christiano2017,Ouyang2022}. Large language models generate
general-purpose text~\citep{BrownGPT3_2020}, and code models and tool-using agents
also emit executable programs or action
traces~\citep{ChenCodex2021,YaoReAct2023,SchickToolformer2023}.

\subsubsection{What must still be accounted for}

Amortization changes where the computation happens, not whether it must be done.
Data generation and parameter optimization can outweigh the time saved at inference,
so a complete cost comparison counts training alongside later evaluations. And the
strategic property of the output still rests on whatever analytic or post-training check the method applies: amortization
moves the work, but the guarantee is a separate cost.

That guarantee, in turn, depends on access. A high-dimensional rule may be
reachable only through queries, where finite tests cover sampled instances and
representable alternatives; extending such results to a larger deviation class
requires a bound or a structural argument. Executable code, or a rule confined to
a known family, may instead admit symbolic checking or a direct theorem.

In short, construction and checking are separate costs. Learning can amortize
the construction across related instances, but it does not by itself establish
the target property over the full deviation class.
Sections~\ref{sec:ai4gt} and~\ref{sec:gt4ai} identify, domain by domain, exactly
which additional argument that missing guarantee requires.
