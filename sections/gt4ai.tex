\section{Game Theory for AI}
\label{sec:gt4ai}

Evaluation places a learned policy against either an opponent population or an attack class, which
fixes the scope of the result. A game designed for training must also represent
the external objective it is meant to serve. In deployed markets, participants'
responses can change the cases encountered, and the institution determines
whose outcomes count.

\subsection{Strategic Evaluation against Opponent Populations}
\label{sec:gt4ai:evaluation}

A competence score is defined relative to an opponent population and an
interaction protocol, including the strategy space available to each player. A
fixed benchmark applies the same cases to every model, whereas an adaptive
opponent helps determine the effective test distribution. The evaluator can
query the tested policy in new interactions but must use a finite population as
a surrogate for future opponents. A policy may therefore perform well against
one population and fail against another that exploits it.

Externally assigned payoffs support comparisons within the represented
population and deviation class. If the model's choices are instead interpreted
as evidence of its own utility, they must also support a stable preference
representation. This additional requirement is unnecessary when the evaluator
stipulates the payoffs.

\subsubsection{Empirical strategic populations}
The dependence on the opponent can be expressed directly. Let \(\pi\) denote the policy under evaluation, let \(\sigma\) denote an opponent policy, and let \(u(\pi,\sigma)\) be the expected payoff to \(\pi\) under a specified interaction protocol. A conventional evaluation score against an opponent distribution \(q\) has the form
\[
S(\pi;q)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma\sim q}
\left[
u(\pi,\sigma)
\right].
\]
Changing \(q\) can alter both the magnitude of the score and the ranking of the evaluated policies. A benchmark containing many strategically similar opponents can overweight one region of the strategy space, while omission of an exploiting opponent can inflate average performance.

Balduzzi et al.\ identify this dependence in reinforcement-learning evaluation,
where a growing collection of environments and baselines can fragment the
resulting evidence~\cite{Balduzzi2018}. Different opponent selections can reverse
the apparent ranking of two agents. A game-theoretic scalar summary derives its
meaning from a population game: the evaluator specifies the included policies
and sampling procedure, then chooses a solution concept that aggregates the
payoff relations.

Empirical game-theoretic analysis provides the basic representation. Given a
finite population \(\Pi=\{\pi_m,\; m\in\mathcal{M}\}\) of policies indexed by
\(\mathcal{M}\), the evaluator estimates the payoff associated with each relevant
joint policy profile through simulation or recorded interactions. These estimates define an
empirical game in which each policy in \(\Pi\) is a strategy~\cite{Wellman2006}.
For a symmetric two-player setting, the empirical payoff matrix may be written as
\[
\widehat{A}_{ij}
=
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}
\left[
u(\pi_i,\pi_j)
\right].
\]
The matrix preserves pairwise strategic relationships that an average benchmark
score would suppress. Within the selected population, it can reveal dominance
relations, cycles, and local strengths, subject to sampling error in its entries.

\subsubsection{Coverage of deviations}
Exploitability makes this dependence explicit. For a strategy profile \(s\), a deviation-class-specific Nash-convergence measure can be written as
\[
\operatorname{NashConv}_{\mathcal{D}}(s)
=
\sum_i
\left[
\sup_{s_i'\in\mathcal{D}_i}
u_i(s_i',s_{-i})
-
u_i(s)
\right],
\]
where \(\mathcal{D}_i\) is the class of deviations available to player \(i\)
and is assumed to include the current strategy. The quantity is zero when no
player can improve within the specified class. If \(\mathcal{D}_i\) contains
every feasible strategy, the expression recovers the full
no-profitable-deviation condition. In empirical evaluation, the supremum is
usually replaced by a maximum over a finite policy population or by the output
of an approximate best-response procedure. The reported value is then
restricted exploitability: it measures the largest gain available among the
deviations that the evaluator was able to represent and query.

Restricted exploitability tests whether the represented class contains a
profitable counterstrategy, whereas win rate averages outcomes under a specified
opponent distribution. A low restricted value is weaker evidence than a low
full-game exploitability bound: the evaluator may omit the relevant
deviation, or an approximate best-response learner may fail to discover it.

Policy-space response oracles update the population iteratively. PSRO
alternates between solving the empirical meta-game and training approximate best
responses to its current meta-strategy~\cite{Lanctot2017}. Each response query
attempts to expose a strategic weakness in the existing population. A successful
response expands the population and updates the empirical game. The current
population defines the test, while the tested profile determines which opponent
should be generated next.

A policy can overfit its training opponents, scoring well under the training
distribution yet failing under a different mixture or against a response absent
from that population. Adaptive population updates reduce this risk to the extent
that the response learner can search an adequate policy class.

\subsubsection{Verdicts within the represented population}
Once an empirical game has been constructed, different solution concepts induce
different evaluation verdicts. Nash averaging computes a maximum-entropy Nash
distribution and evaluates policies against that strategically selected
mixture~\cite{Balduzzi2018}. Redundant opponents and policies exploitable by the
equilibrium population receive less weight. The resulting score is tied to
best-response conditions in the represented population, so adding a
strategically relevant policy can change both the equilibrium mixture and the
resulting ordering.

A further difficulty is non-transitivity. In a transitive population, policies can be arranged approximately along a single strength dimension: stronger policies tend to defeat weaker ones. Conventional rating systems are useful in this setting. In a non-transitive population, pairwise performance may instead form a cycle,
\[
\pi_A \succ \pi_B,\qquad
\pi_B \succ \pi_C,\qquad
\pi_C \succ \pi_A.
\]
No scalar ordering preserves all three relations. Reporting only average performance can make the ranking depend on the frequency assigned to each opponent, while selecting one policy as universally strongest discards the strategic structure that determines its vulnerabilities.

\(\alpha\)-Rank addresses this setting through an evolutionary model over strategy profiles~\cite{Omidshafiei2019}. It constructs a Markov chain in which transitions represent the fixation of strategically advantageous mutations and uses the stationary distribution to rank policies or joint profiles. Because the method operates on the directed response structure of the empirical game, it can represent cycles and recurrent strategic components that a transitive rating would collapse. Its output remains population-relative, and the state space grows rapidly with the number of agents and strategies. The stochastic-optimization approach of \(\alpha^{\alpha}\)-Rank addresses this scalability problem in large empirical games~\cite{Yang2020}.

Open-ended learning provides another account of the same structure. Balduzzi et al. separate transitive progress from non-transitive strategic diversity in symmetric zero-sum games~\cite{Balduzzi2019Open}. Along the transitive component, one policy can improve in a way that generalizes across much of the population. Along a non-transitive component, improvement is meaningful only relative to the opponents currently represented. A learner may discover a policy that defeats the incumbent without being globally stronger, because another existing or future policy defeats the new response.

The spinning-top model extends this observation beyond simple cyclic examples. Czarnecki et al. argue that many real-world games combine a broad transitive direction with substantial non-transitive variation around policies of similar overall strength~\cite{Czarnecki2020}. Weak policies may be defeated by many alternatives, while increasingly capable policies enter a wider region containing diverse specialists and counterstrategies. Evaluation near the frontier therefore requires both progress along the transitive dimension and coverage of the surrounding non-transitive population. A single champion policy or scalar rating can conceal this strategic diversity.

\subsubsection{Game-playing systems and language models as evaluated policies}
These population-level ideas already shape deployed game-playing systems.
AlphaStar's final strategic output is a Nash-weighted mixture of selected league
agents, intended to reduce exploitability across that
population~\cite{Vinyals2019}. Its robustness depends on the complementary
policies in the mixture. Imperfect-information poker systems use a related
evaluation standard.
DeepStack reports local best-response measurements to estimate how much an
opponent could gain by exploiting its strategy~\cite{Moravcik2017}. Libratus and
Pluribus combine performance against strong human players with game-theoretic
analysis of their strategies and abstractions~\cite{BrownSandholm2018,Brown2019Pluribus}.
Match results establish practical strength against the encountered opponents.
Exploitability or local-best-response analysis asks whether a strategically
selected counterpolicy could obtain a systematic advantage.

The same evaluation vocabulary applies when the strategic subject is a language
model. A model engaged in a strategic exchange defines a conditional policy
over messages and actions. Its behavior depends on both the problem statement
and the interaction history, including the counterparty and protocol. A
benchmark that aggregates outcomes across games may produce a convenient
summary, but that summary does not identify which strategic structures the
model handles successfully.

GTBench evaluates language models across game-theoretic settings and reports
that their strategic reasoning varies with the structure of the
game~\cite{GTBench2024}. Its results identify task-specific capabilities and
failure modes rather than a general level of game-theoretic competence.
Repeated-game experiments make the opponent dependence more direct. Akata et
al. show that language-model cooperation and coordination vary with the prompt,
opponent, and interaction history~\cite{Akata2025}. In their experiments, GPT-4
behaves unforgivingly in the Prisoner's Dilemma but fails to coordinate reliably
with an alternating opponent in Battle of the Sexes. Neither observation is
captured by simply calling the model cooperative or rational. Its apparent
strategic character changes with the interaction rule and the behavior to which
it must respond.

These studies treat the language model as a policy and measure its competence
through play. Strong performance supports competence within the benchmark, and
a failure can refute a broader claim. Claims about other interactions require
separate evidence.

\subsubsection{Preference interpretation when utility is inferred}
When an evaluation assigns payoffs externally, best response, regret, and
exploitability are defined relative to those payoffs without making a claim
about the agent's own preferences. A separate question arises when model
choices are interpreted as evidence of an internal utility or preference. If
those choices reverse under strategically irrelevant changes in wording or
framing, there may be no stable preference representation to recover.
Preference-coherence tests determine whether observed choices support this
interpretation.

Classical utility representation relies on conditions such as completeness and transitivity. Completeness requires the agent to compare the relevant alternatives, while transitivity excludes unresolved cycles. A random-utility model can separate a persistent latent ordering from noisy observations, so stochastic choice need not violate either condition. Repeated choices must nevertheless admit a stable deterministic or stochastic representation that transfers across elicitation settings.

Before choices can be read as revealing a utility, they must be stable enough to
reveal anything. Ross, Kim, and Lo test language models for behavioral patterns associated with inequity aversion, risk and loss attitudes, and time discounting~\cite{RossKimLo2024}. The models studied are neither uniformly human-like nor uniformly consistent with the benchmark of an idealized economic agent. Their measured consistency changes across decisions and settings. The study also applies a competence screen before interpreting the economic tests and analyzes only models that pass it. This procedure makes the two-stage structure explicit: an evaluator first determines whether a model understands and can execute the decision task, and then asks whether its choices support a coherent utility interpretation.

Utility Engineering fits Thurstonian random-utility models directly to AI
preference data~\cite{Mazeika2025}. The framework tests whether observed
comparisons admit complete and transitive latent preferences while allowing
stochastic choice. The reported association between model scale and preference
coherence supports utility-based analysis for the evaluated models and settings.

Behavioral-economics comparisons provide related but distinct evidence. Mei et al. compare chatbot behavior with human behavior across classic experiments and report that GPT-4 is statistically difficult to distinguish from humans in the settings studied. When it does differ, its behavior tends to be more cooperative~\cite{Mei2024}. Other work uses language models as simulated economic agents or as populations intended to reproduce human-subject studies~\cite{Horton2023,Aher2023}. Behavioral similarity can support particular simulation uses, but it does not establish preference coherence by itself. A model may reproduce an aggregate human response distribution while changing its choices across equivalent framings, and a coherent model may hold preferences that differ systematically from those of humans.

Preference-coherence tests concern a stable utility representation. Empirical
game metrics evaluate a policy under stipulated payoffs and an opponent
population. Win rate, Nash averaging, \(\alpha\)-Rank, and exploitability
aggregate that population differently.

Some of these diagnostics can be moved into training. An adversary can search
for exploitable responses, and an interactive protocol can expose answers that a
fixed output score cannot judge. The next subsection studies such designed
games.


\subsubsection{Adversarial robustness}

An attack set serves the same evaluative role as an opponent population: it
defines the class against which robustness is measured. GANs provide one
game-theoretic example: a generator and a discriminator play a zero-sum minimax
game whose intended Nash point leaves the discriminator unable to separate
generated from real data~\cite{Goodfellow2014}. The attacker's feasible actions
differ across applications.
Madry et al.\ fix a bounded perturbation set in advance and approximate the
resulting constrained inner maximization with projected gradient descent
~\cite{Madry2018}. LLM red teaming uses a language model to search a wider space
of attacks~\cite{Perez2022}. Multi-round variants
allow newly discovered attacks to shape the next defense by co-evolving attacker
and defender populations~\cite{MaYang2024RTG}.

An $\ell_p$ guarantee covers perturbations inside the specified ball. A learned
red-team population has an analogous scope, determined by the deviations its
policy class can represent and its optimization procedure can discover. PSRO
makes the adaptive search explicit by adding approximate best responses and
solving the resulting meta-game~\cite{Lanctot2017}. Its meta-strategy is a distribution over distinct
policies or networks, whereas a stochastic LLM policy directly represents a
conditional distribution over responses. At LLM scale, the former may require
several sets of weights or a later distillation step.

Even a rich attacker population leaves the equilibrium dynamics unresolved.
Farnia and Ozdaglar construct parameterized GAN games with no local Nash
equilibrium and give first-order dynamics that fail to converge in the last
iterate~\cite{FarniaOzdaglar2020}. Their construction is specific to GANs, yet
it is enough to rule out any general inference from minimax form alone:
equilibrium existence and convergence of first-order dynamics require separate
arguments.

Certified defenses make explicit the distinction between what is covered and what is claimed
by returning a radius within which no perturbation can change the
prediction~\cite{CohenRosenfeldKolter2019}. In evidentiary terms, the certificate
plays a role analogous to a duality bound in
Section~\ref{sec:ai4gt:equilibrium}: it supplies a theorem-backed guarantee over
a stated class.

\medskip
\noindent
In each setting, adaptive search can broaden the tested class. A claim about the
full target still requires either exhaustive coverage or an argument controlling
unsearched alternatives.


\subsection{Designed Games as Evidence}
\label{sec:gt4ai:training}

Training and oversight objectives are often defined relative to another
participant. Robustness uses an attack set, preference learning uses comparisons
between responses, and an oversight protocol uses a judge with specified
capabilities. The modeled counterparty and its information therefore determine
which failures the game can expose. We examine preference games, which aggregate
pairwise comparisons, and interactive-proof games, which use a bounded verifier.

Finite training covers only part of the modeled participants' behavior, and
deployment may elicit responses absent from the training game. The game's
strategy spaces and information structure determine its target equilibrium,
while the learning dynamics determine which policy is reached. A system-level
claim requires both the relevant participant class and the external objective to
be represented adequately.

\subsubsection{Preference games: what the aggregation certifies}

Consider three responses whose pairwise
majority relation satisfies
$A \succ B$, $B \succ C$, and $C \succ A$. No scalar ranking preserves all
three comparisons. Bradley--Terry models cannot represent this cycle in the
induced majority relation because latent scalar scores impose a transitive
ordering, although stable cycles arise in psychological, social-choice, and
online-comparison
settings~\cite{BradleyTerry1952,May1954,Tversky1969,Dudik2015}. A preference
game retains the pairwise relation and lets the solution concept perform the
aggregation. Nash learning from human feedback (NLHF) takes this route. It
represents the learned comparison model as a symmetric
two-player game and adds KL regularization relative to a reference
policy~\cite{Munos2024}. With tabular policies, the last iterate of Nash-MD
converges to the unique regularized Nash equilibrium at an $O(1/T)$ rate in KL
divergence. The equilibrium balances pairwise payoff against the cost of moving
away from the reference policy. A competitor may beat it under the raw
comparison matrix and still fail to improve the regularized objective.

The KL term changes the target and the dynamics. In a zero-sum game without a
payoff-level KL term, regularized-learning and mirror-type dynamics may converge
in time average while their last iterates cycle~\cite{Mertikopoulos2018}. Nash-MD
instead solves a preference game whose payoff already includes the KL penalty.
Optimistic mirror descent and extragradient-style updates obtain last-iterate
guarantees under different assumptions~\cite{Mertikopoulos2019}. These results
concern different games and different iterates. A convergence claim has to name
both.

Recent LLM methods preserve the equilibrium perspective while changing the
optimization. These differ in optimizer rather than in target: INPO packages no-regret self-play
into a single loss, DNO uses an
iterative direct-preference update, and SPPO applies Hedge to a constant-sum
comparison game~\cite{Zhang2025INPO,Rosset2024DNO,Wu2024SPPO}.
SPO/Minimaximalist changes the solution target by adopting the social-choice
Minimax Winner. It constructs instances in which the standard reward-based pipelines --- reinforcement
learning from human feedback (RLHF) and direct preference optimization (DPO) ---
do not recover that solution~\cite{Swamy2024SPO}. Its experiments are in
continuous control, so they do not directly establish the same failure in
LLM fine-tuning. The choice of target remains substantive. Minimax Winners and
regularized Nash equilibria aggregate preferences differently, and successful
optimization does not decide which aggregation is normatively appropriate.

\subsubsection{Interactive-proof games: what a bounded judge can establish}

A weak judge may be able to check
one disputed step even when the full proof lies beyond its competence. Debate
and Prover--Verifier Games exploit this gap by arranging an interaction that
brings locally checkable claims before the judge. Recursive reward modeling,
iterated amplification, and process supervision pursue the same oversight goal
through other decompositions.

Complexity theory explains why the interaction can add power. Irving,
Christiano, and Amodei use the
\textsc{Debate}$=$\textsc{Pspace} analogy: with optimal play and polynomially
many alternating rounds, a polynomial-time judge can decide \textsc{Pspace}
problems, while direct checking of a polynomial-length static witness
corresponds to \textsc{NP}~\cite{Irving2018}. The statement differs from the
single-prover identity \textsc{IP}$=$\textsc{Pspace}. Alternating debate
relativizes to oracle access, which supplies the complexity-theoretic reason for
a second prover when the human judge is available only as a black-box oracle.

Anil et al.\ show that this promise depends on turn order. In their
prover-leading sequential game, an optimal prover can make its message
uninformative about the true label. A verifier-leading protocol can recover
desirable completeness and soundness properties under the prescribed
information order, whereas the simultaneous game may admit equilibria in which
the verifier ignores the prover~\cite{Anil2021}. The failure is information
suppression: the prover removes label information from its message instead of
overwhelming the verifier with additional evidence.

Kirchner et al.\ turn checkability into a training objective by teaching LLM
provers to produce reasoning that weaker verifiers can assess~\cite{Kirchner2024}.
Their experiments also exhibit a legibility tax: gains in checkability can come
with lower answer accuracy than under correctness-only training.
A more severe problem arises when an incorrect argument is easy to state and
computationally expensive to refute~\cite{BarnesChristiano2020}.
Doubly-efficient debate controls the cost on both sides under a restricted
problem model: the honest strategy uses only polynomially more computation than
solving the task requires, and the verifier makes a fixed number of human-judgment
queries~\cite{BrownCohen2023}. The result assumes a polynomial-length record
that a human could verify through extensive reflection, with additional
regularity conditions in the stochastic setting. Prover-estimator debate
addresses obfuscation more directly. Under stability assumptions, it gives the
honest participant a strategy whose computational efficiency is comparable to
that of its opponent~\cite{BrownCohen2025ProverEstimator}.

The theoretical result makes an empirical prediction about current models. Khan
et al.\ test it using inference-time best-of-$N$ and critique-and-refine. They find that stronger
debaters improve the accuracy of both model and human judges on difficult
reading-comprehension questions~\cite{Khan2024}. The consultancy control makes the result
interpretable: increasing a consultant's persuasiveness reduces judge accuracy,
whereas competition between opposing debaters improves it.

Two later studies narrow the empirical claim. Single-turn debate-style
explanations do not improve human accuracy in the setting studied by Parrish et
al.~\cite{Parrish2022}. Across a wider task set, Kenton et al.\ find that debate
consistently outperforms assigned-role consultancy, while comparisons with
direct question answering are mixed outside extractive question answering with
information asymmetry~\cite{Kenton2024}. Debate works under identifiable
conditions in these studies: the judge must benefit from information revealed
by the interaction, and the task must admit a useful decomposition.

A learner may optimize the objective defined by the game while producing behavior the
designer did not want because the game misstated what counts as success. Even an appropriate
game can fail when its learning dynamics do not reach the intended solution. For
interactive-proof games, the evaluator's observations and verification capacity
further limit what the protocol can establish.

Practical value also depends on cost. Population methods must train and
maintain several models. Preference self-play and debate add repeated
generations and evaluator calls. The practical question is whether the
resulting gains in coverage or checkability justify the additional computation.

In markets and delegation chains, the interaction is an external institution
shared by multiple autonomous actors rather than a training component for one
model.


\subsection{Deployment Governance: From Local Objectives to System Outcomes}
\label{sec:gt4ai:governance}

An agent can faithfully implement its principal's objective while the system in
which it acts remains misaligned. Algorithmic pricing makes this possibility
concrete: even when each agent maximizes its firm's profit and adapts
successfully to its rivals, their interaction can sustain supra-competitive
prices and reduce consumer surplus. No individual principal--agent relationship
need have failed. The failure appears only when the locally successful agents
are evaluated together.

Governance must therefore specify a system objective and decide whose outcomes
count. It can alter learning rules and information before deployment or audit
realized behavior afterward. Recent proposals use seller data to construct
statistical tests of algorithmic pricing and state conditions under which an
algorithm passes~\cite{HartlineLongZhang2024,HartlineWangZhang2025}. Harrington
separately proposes making collusion by autonomous pricing agents unlawful
~\cite{Harrington2018}. In each proposal, the audit or legal standard defines the
admissible alternatives and the evidentiary burden. That choice in turn
determines what data are required.

\subsubsection{System objective and affected parties}
A local equilibrium or regret guarantee states a property inside the specified
interaction. A system-level claim additionally requires an argument about the
affected parties and the institutional objective.

Delegation games locate this compositional problem within principal--agent
relations. Sourbut et al.\ distinguish control failures from cooperation
failures and decompose each into alignment and capability components, taking
principal welfare as the evaluative object~\cite{Sourbut2024}. Because
principal welfare depends on both individual control and collective
cooperation, a property established for one principal--agent pair leaves the
collective component unconstrained. Pricing adds an affected group outside this
accounting: consumers are third parties beyond the principals represented in the
delegation game.
Coordination among profit-aligned pricing agents can consequently be a
cooperation success for the firms and a system failure once consumer welfare
enters the objective.

\subsubsection{Ex ante design of learning and information}
The algorithmic-pricing literature shows how strongly market outcomes depend on
the learning protocol. Calvano et al.\ find that Q-learning agents in repeated
differentiated-product Bertrand competition with logit demand learn
supra-competitive prices without direct communication. In their simulations,
the learned strategies exhibit punishment phases followed by a return to high
prices, although the analysis does not establish a general convergence theorem
for Q-learning~\cite{Calvano2020}. Asker et al.\ isolate one source of this
design sensitivity. When the algorithms place no positive weight on future
profits, perfect synchronous updating (which computes counterfactual returns
for unchosen prices) produces competitive pricing in their baseline
environment, whereas asynchronous updating can produce prices near the monopoly
level. Giving positive weight to future profits also permits supra-competitive
prices under synchronous updating, although the effect is smaller than under
asynchronous updating~\cite{Asker2022}. Banchio and Skrzypacz study the same
sensitivity in a repeated auction with two bidders, equal normalized values, and
a discrete bid grid. Without additional feedback, first-price auctions sustain
bids below the model's competitive benchmark. Information about the lowest
winning bid increases competitiveness~\cite{BanchioSkrzypacz2022}.

Cartea et al.\ give a theoretical result for a restricted class. They establish a
Folk-theorem-style result for boundedly rational learners using finite-memory
strategies in repeated potential games, so the result does not transfer
directly to general differentiated Bertrand
competition~\cite{Cartea2025}. Fish et al.\ study LLM agents:
in their oligopoly experiments, the agents quickly and autonomously reach
supra-competitive prices, and seemingly innocuous prompt changes alter the
degree of supra-competitive pricing~\cite{FishGonczarowskiShorrer2024}. Assad et
al.\ add field evidence from non-monopoly German retail gasoline markets.
Using headquarters-level adoption as an instrument, they estimate that
algorithmic pricing raises margins. In duopoly and triopoly markets, margins
rise when all competing stations adopt~\cite{Assad2024}.

The algorithm label alone does not determine the market behavior to be
evaluated. The update and information rules give ex ante standards concrete
design targets. Ex post audits test whether the resulting deployment satisfies
the relevant behavioral standard.

\subsubsection{Ex post standards and deviation classes}
The deviation class determines what a behavioral audit can exclude.
External regret compares realized utility with the utility from using one fixed
action throughout. Internal regret evaluates the counterfactual replacement of
a chosen action $i$ by another action $j$, while swap regret permits a separate
replacement for every chosen action.
For a finite price set $\mathcal{P}$, calibrated regret specializes swap regret
to posted prices. It is the maximum average gain over all remappings
$\sigma:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}$, where each posted price $p$ is
replaced by $\sigma(p)$ while the demand environment for that round is held
fixed~\cite{HartlineLongZhang2024}. Low calibrated regret therefore rules out
a profitable price-contingent remapping.

When every player has vanishing internal regret, the empirical joint-action
distribution approaches the set of correlated equilibria of the stage
game~\cite{FosterVohra1997,HartMasColell2000,BlumMansour2007}. This theorem
restricts conditional incentives in the empirical distribution. It contains no
welfare or legal criterion. Calling the resulting distribution competitive
therefore requires a benchmark external to the equilibrium theorem.

Hartline, Long, and Zhang supply such a benchmark by defining unilateral
non-collusion through calibrated best response~\cite{HartlineLongZhang2024}.
Conditional on the information revealed by a seller's price, that price must
approximately maximize the seller's expected profit in the induced demand
environment. The conditioning matters because variation in posted prices can
reveal information on which the seller acts. Low calibrated regret is the
dynamic analogue of this condition over a deployment history, and when every
seller satisfies calibrated best response, the joint price distribution forms
a correlated equilibrium of the stage pricing game. Hartline's subsequent
account designates stage-game equilibrium prices as competitive and additional
repeated-game equilibria as non-competitive~\cite{Hartline2026}. This
convention gives the deviation condition an operational interpretation. The
correlated-equilibrium result itself has no general implication for consumer
surplus. Market assumptions and the welfare or legal benchmark remain separate
inputs.

The implications of a no-regret guarantee depend on its deviation class.
Braverman et al.\ show that a seller facing a buyer who uses EXP3 or another
mean-based no-regret algorithm can extract nearly the buyer's full welfare. The
same paper constructs a different no-regret algorithm for which repeatedly
posting the Myerson reserve is optimal for the seller~\cite{Braverman2018}.
Thus two algorithms can both have low external
regret while exposing the buyer to very different strategic losses. Hartline,
Wang, and Zhang show similarly that
algorithms satisfying only best-in-hindsight regret can be manipulated into
posting supra-competitive prices even when neither seller has side
information~\cite{HartlineWangZhang2025}. External regret can miss this
vulnerability because it compares the realized sequence only with a single
fixed action used throughout. A profitable deviation may instead be
conditional---replacing a price $p$ by $q$ only on rounds when $p$ was
posted---without any single fixed price improving the overall average.
Calibrated regret tests these price-contingent replacements rather than only
fixed-price deviations. Each seller can satisfy this property unilaterally,
regardless of its rivals' algorithms.

\subsubsection{Auditability from deployment records}
Hartline, Long, and Zhang estimate this property from a deployment transcript
containing the posted price, realized demand at that price, and the randomized
price distribution for each round~\cite{HartlineLongZhang2024}. An
inverse-propensity estimator uses the logged action probabilities to estimate
counterfactual demand, and its sample complexity grows as the minimum
probability assigned to a price decreases. A pricing algorithm can therefore
add exploration and logging so that a competitive deployment becomes
auditable. The estimator operates without source-code access and depends on
credible internal records of randomization and outcomes. This is a logged
black-box audit whose validity depends on the provenance and integrity of the
transcript.

When the record is incomplete, the available conclusion may be a bound rather
than a point verdict. Pessimistic calibrated regret relaxes the original full-support
requirement~\cite{HartlineWangZhang2025}. When the transcript leaves some
counterfactual outcomes unidentified, the refined method takes the highest
calibrated regret over all outcome completions compatible with the observed
data. A low pessimistic value certifies low regret for every compatible
completion, whereas a high value may reflect either a genuine profitable
deviation or the missing information itself. The authors interpret failure to
supply a low-regret certificate as a violation of a proposed per se
rule, thereby placing the evidentiary burden on the seller. That allocation of
burden is a legal choice.

Unknown costs create another identification boundary.
If the auditor permits marginal cost to lie in a set $C$, a seller passes the
plausible-non-collusion test whenever some $c\in C$ makes the observed behavior
exhibit low regret, even if the true cost would imply high regret. Higher
imputed costs can thus rationalize supra-competitive prices generated under a
lower true cost, and the test becomes less informative as $C$ expands. The
available cost evidence and governing legal standard determine how wide that
rationalizing set may be.

\subsubsection{From individual evidence to market-level conclusions}
Arunachaleswaran et al.\ study sequential deployment in which
the first seller commits to any no-regret algorithm and the second seller
optimizes in the induced environment. Supra-competitive prices arise whenever
the second seller obtains at least the payoff of random pricing, including when
it optimizes only over non-responsive price distributions. They also construct
an algorithm-space Nash equilibrium with near-monopoly prices and no explicitly
encoded threats~\cite{Arunachaleswaran2025}. Hartline classifies this outcome as
non-competitive while rejecting the collusion label: one seller learns a best
response, whereas the other uses the non-competitive static
strategy~\cite{HartlineClarification2026}. The outcome violates the stage-game
competitive benchmark, yet the evidence falls short of establishing joint
collusion. Low calibrated regret is an individual-level condition. The
market-level correlated-equilibrium conclusion follows only when every seller
satisfies it. If one seller fails, that market-level conclusion no
longer follows. Whether antitrust law should prohibit high-regret or naively
non-optimizing pricing when agreement is unproven remains a policy choice. By
itself, a failed audit does not establish an agreement among
sellers~\cite{Harrington2018}.

Mechanism design changes the update or information structure before deployment,
whereas regret auditing evaluates realized behavior afterward. When every seller has
low calibrated regret, the data impose a stage-game equilibrium restriction on
the market outcome. That restriction does not determine whether the outcome is
socially or legally acceptable. Evaluating system alignment requires an explicit
objective for each agent and a separate criterion for the market outcome. A
seller can optimize its assigned objective even when the resulting market fails
that criterion.
