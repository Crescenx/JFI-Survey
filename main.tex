\begin{abstract}
Learning now enters game theory in two roles: as a computational tool and as a
source of strategic behavior. Training can amortize work across related games,
but a learned strategy or rule remains a candidate until the method establishes
the relevant game-theoretic property. Deviation-based properties can quantify
over alternatives that finite search does not examine. With observational data,
distinct strategic models may remain indistinguishable. A designed game raises a separate
question: whether its model of interaction adequately represents the external
objective. We use these two questions to compare six areas, from equilibrium
computation to algorithmic-pricing governance. In each area, we distinguish the
immediate output from the argument needed to extend finite evaluation to the
target claim. We also consider deployments in which participants respond to a
learned rule and thereby alter the cases encountered after deployment.
\end{abstract}
